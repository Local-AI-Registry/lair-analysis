Cannibalization and semantic search, in plain terms

Cannibalization is when several of your own pages chase the same search. Google will usually rank only one page per site for a search, so your pages split the links, clicks and attention that one page should get. Google often picks the wrong one. Today it shows a short blog post for "bulbous nose" instead of the page that explains Dr. Dugar's procedure.

Semantic search means Google and AI tools no longer match keywords. They work out the topic and judge whether a site covers it completely, clearly and from real expertise. A site that covers rhinoplasty in depth, with every page linking back to one main guide, reads as the authority. A site where rhinoplasty pages sit next to lip plumper recipes and country pages reads as unfocused. ChatGPT and Google's AI Overview make the same judgment when they decide whom to name.

The content the site needs

One rebuilt Rhinoplasty page at the center, with focused pages around it. Each supporting page covers one topic in depth and links back to the center.

Why the core update hit sites that stood still

A core update is not a penalty. Google re-ranks the whole web, comparing every page with every other page that could answer the same search. When competitors improve and a site does not, the site loses ground even if nothing on it broke.

Independent analysis of the May 2026 update found that Google rewarded the page that best fit what the searcher wanted, rather than the biggest or oldest site. That hurts a site like this in three ways:

  1. For "rhinoplasty beverly hills," the best fit is a deep, current page about rhinoplasty in Beverly Hills. This site offers a 2024 page of links.
  2. Pages written for one search but aimed at another market, like "best rhinoplasty in Oman," fit no one well.
  3. Off-topic posts make the whole site look less focused on rhinoplasty.

Your data shows that pattern. Your average position barely moved, but the site stopped appearing for about half the searches it used to. Sites typically recover when the next core updates reassess the improved pages, usually several months after the work is done.
